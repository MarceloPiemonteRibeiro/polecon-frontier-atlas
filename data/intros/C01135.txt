Sustained  growth episodes, as in China following its transition to a mar-  ket economy or Singapore’s version of managed capitalism, are  often attributed to the role of the state in helping, rather than  hindering, private initiative. Occasionally, cumulative spells of  strong growth have led to major economic transformations over  a few generations, lifting millions out of poverty and increasing  well-being. But to whom do citizens attribute economic success,  and how does it manifest itself ? Positive attitudes toward gov-  ernment following such success would suggest that citizens give  credit to their governments for generating it. Moreover, this can  have positive feedback effects onto support for institutions and  policy compliance ( Besley and Dray 2024 ; OECD 2024 ; Acemoglu  et al. 2025 ). 
This article provides evidence that a strong lifetime growth  experience builds trust in government. We show this in a newly  assembled data set of 3.3 million individuals across 166 coun-  tries that harmonizes 11 major opinion surveys with responses  going back to 1990. Following an emerging literature on memory  and economic decisions and exploiting country-cohort variation  in GDP per capita, we calculate respondents’ experiences with  growth since birth using a weighting function where memory de-  cays linearly throughout individuals’ lifetimes ( Malmendier and 
Nagel 2011 , 2016 ; Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer 2020 ). We also  focus on the United States using the American National Election 
Studies (ANES) to gauge trust in the federal government as far  back as 1958, capturing growth experiences and attitudes for in-  dividuals born since the end of the nineteenth century. We also  use the Swiss Household Panel (SHP), which allows us to track  trust in government and growth experiences for the same person  over time. 
Our core empirical strategy leverages variation in growth ex-  periences across birth cohorts, countries, and time controlling for  a range of ﬁxed effects—country, age, cohort and survey waves— as well as individual characteristics and GDP per capita in a re-  spondent’s year of birth. We exploit the fact that differences in  lifetime growth experiences across different cohorts are frequent  within the same country.
