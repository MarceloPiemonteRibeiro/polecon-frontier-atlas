Social relationships are essential to human well-being and play a vital role in the maintenance of health1.
The impact of social isolation (SI) is recognized as a global public health concern, and the World Health
Organization also recognizes the important effect of SI on mortality2. Considerable research attention has been devoted to investigating SI and the potential risk of death, especially in high-income countries3–
6.
SI involves a lack of social connections, which manifests itself in the form of lacking a partner, few interactions with family and friends, and little participation in religious activities, organizations, and clubs7. SI is an objective and quantifiable reflection of a small social network and lack of social contact8
9. Studies conducted in different countries show that the prevalence of SI varies considerably10. Older adults are more likely to experience conditions like transitioning to retirement and an accompanying loss of roles, poor health, loss of a spouse or friends, and limited mobility11, which are strongly associated with the risk of mortality. Moreover, SI has been suggested to be associated with poorer health behaviors12, including smoking and physical inactivity, as well as poorer sleep, suicide, and health-related biological processes13 14 such as higher C-reactive protein levels and lipid profiles, and poorer immune function. Consequently, SI has been suggested to be an independent predictor of mortality that is on a par with conventional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption15.
Dozens of studies using various measures of SI have examined the associations between SI and the risk of mortality; however, the findings remain controversial. Some studies have found a higher risk of mortality from all causes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer associated with SI16–18, whereas others have reported non-significant results19–21. In 2015, the only meta-analysis to date examining loneliness, SI, and all-cause mortality22 found SI to be an influencing factor, with a 29% increased risk
(95% confidence interval [CI], 1.06 to 1.56) of mortality from all causes. Many subsequent studies have been carried out on the relationship between SI and mortality, but the results have been inconsistent8 18
19. Additionally, no systematic review of an association between SI and the risk of mortality in patient populations (eg, patients with CVD or cancer) has been conducted. Therefore, we conducted an updated and more comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies to summarize the associations between SI and risk of mortality from all causes, CVD, and cancer, especially in the general population and a subset of patients with cardiovascular diseases or breast/ colorectal malignancies.

Methods
The findings from this systematic review and meta-analysis were reported based on the Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA)23 guideline (Table S1). The protocol of the study was registered with PROSPERO (reg. NO CRD42022299959).
Data sources and database search
We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase for studies published in English between database inception and October 2021. Appendix 1 provides details of the search terms used. Manual retrieval and a review of the reference lists of retrieved studies were also performed to avoid missing eligible studies.
Inclusion and exclusion criteria
All article titles and abstracts were first reviewed independently by two authors (Y.G. and Z.H.). Study inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) studies including adults aged more than 18 years; 2) studies with a prospective observational design; 3) studies reporting effect sizes using hazard ratio (HR), relative risk
(RR), or odds ratio (OR), with the corresponding 95% CI, for the association between SI as the exposure of interest and mortality from all causes, CVD, or specific cancers as the outcome. Diagnoses were classified according to the World Health Organization International Classification of Diseases (ICD)24 25. If multiple studies reported data from the same population, only the study with the largest sample size was included. The exclusion criteria were: 1) commentaries, editorials, letters, reviews, or meta-analyses; 2) studies in which SI was not considered as an independent variable; 3) studies performed among children, adolescents, or patients with HIV; 4) studies reporting effect sizes of mixed risk factors for disease and mortality.
We included all studies with SI evaluated using different methods (detailed definitions in Table S2) in the original studies. Most studies used the Social Network Index (SNI), a measure of SI created by Berkman and Syme7, used to evaluate an individual’s marital or partnership relationships, support from friends or family, and religious or other group participation. Interviews are conducted to assess these factors, and responses are combined into a social network index metric on a scale of 0 to 4 (0 being the highest level of isolation)926. Other studies used a revised version of the SNI (with questions modified according to the cohort characteristics) as a measure of SI 1727. Additionally, some studies used surveys to assess SI; for example, Sakurai et al.19 assessed SI via a widely used questionnaire2829. In the survey, respondents were asked about the frequency of both face-to-face and non-face-to-face interactions (e.g., telephone, e-mail, letters) with family members who did not live with them, relatives, friends, and neighbors. SI was defined as having contact with anyone less than once a week2829.
