History is full of examples of authoritarian regimes affecting people’s beliefs and preferences:  dictators face little checks and balances, spread propaganda and control media and educational  systems, and recur to sheer violent repression and coercion to stay in power. Research in social  psychology suggests that exposure to dictatorships may affect preferences. If political regimes  have an impact on citizens’ preferences, they can have long-term effects on a country’s outcomes  through citizens’ choices after a regime change: individuals who grew up during a dictatorship  might vote, in a democracy, following preferences partly shaped by exposure to a dictatorship. 
Preferences and beliefs may affect long-term economic outcomes directly (Guiso, Sapienza and 
Zingales 2006) or indirectly through political institutions (Besley and Kudamatsu 2008; Mulligan, Gil  and Sala-i Martin 2004). Then, what is the effect of exposure to dictatorships on long-term political  beliefs and preferences? Will individuals’ political ideas be permanently affected by dictators’  actions? To shed light on these questions, I match self-reported preferences and beliefs with the  political regime under which individuals lived during their childhood and early youth. Considering 
18 Latin American countries in the period spanning 1995–2010, I use the variation in exposure to  dictatorships by country and cohort of birth to identify, in a differences-in-differences setting, the  effect of exposure on a set of outcome variables capturing preferences over political regimes, trust  in institutions, and ideological orientation. 
Focus on childhood and youth stems from psychological theories on preference and belief  formation. As an empirically backed theory originally developed in the sixties, the Impressionable 
Years Hypothesis (IYH) states that individuals are more malleable during youth: core values are  formed during this period and experience little to no change afterwards (Cutler 1974; Easton and 
Dennis 1980; Greenstein 1965; Hess and Torney 1967; Krosnick and Alwin 1989; Sears 1975).1 There  is no clear consensus on the length of the impressionable years: evidence of political socialization  has been found for ages 18 to 25 (Newcomb et al. 1967), as well as 10 to 17 (Krosnick and Alwin 
1989) and 4 to 18 (Madestam and Yanagizawa-Drott 2011). In a similar line, the work of economist 
James Heckman and co-authors has shown that the effect of shocks and stimuli on skill and  personality development is greater during early childhood and decreases over time (Cunha and 
Heckman 2007; Gertler et al. 2014; Heckman 2011; Heckman, Pinto and Savelyev 2013; Knudsen  et al. 2006). Within economics, the IYH lies behind recent studies of preferences over redistribution 
(Alesina and Fuchs-Schundeln 2007) and political partisanship (Madestam and Yanagizawa-Drott 
2011), while in political science, it is the starting point for studies on party affiliation and voting 
(Sears and Valentino 1997; Valentino and Sears 1998). 
I focus on exposure to dictatorships for two reasons. First, non-democratic regimes have  greater incentives than democracies to affect individuals’ preferences, while they also face lower  constraints. For dictators, being ousted from power may involve severe economic loss and even  prison or death, while the benefits and rents of remaining in power tend to be higher than those  perceived by government leaders or policy makers in democratic regimes. Thus, dictators have  greater incentives to try and affect individuals’ preferences to legitimate themselves and their  regimes and increase their probability of survival. In democratic regimes, governments’ actions  are constrained by a set of checks and balances (the constitution and law, separation of powers,  etc.), while different views are represented in parliament through different parties. Government  actions are under scrutiny of a free press, while freedom of speech guarantees that dissenting  opinions can be heard. However, beyond incentives, democratic governments cannot arbitrarily  coerce or incarcerate individuals, whereas non-democratic regimes are mostly characterized by  the use of coercion, incarceration, and forced disappearance of individuals; freedom of speech  is curtailed, parties are banned, and government propaganda is hard to avoid or counter. 
Thus, there are enough differences between regimes themselves that the mere exposure to a  dictatorship (even when the dictator does not try to affect preferences) may have a lasting impact  on individuals.
